CHAPTER ONE

The forest was alive with light.

Sunlight poured through the canopy in golden shafts, dappling the mossy floor in shifting patterns of green and amber. Birds sang somewhere high above, and the air smelled of damp earth and wildflowers and something ancient—something that had been growing and breathing and living here long before anyone thought to give it a name.

It was beautiful.

It was also terrified.

Because somewhere in that beauty, bare feet were pounding against the earth.

Small feet. Dirty feet. Feet that had been running for so long they'd gone numb, that had stopped feeling the twigs and stones and roots cutting into soft skin.

The girl crashed through a curtain of hanging vines, her breath coming in ragged gasps. Her hair—what had once been a neat braid—had come loose and wild, tangled with leaves and sweat. Her dress, a simple cotton thing that might have been yellow once, was torn at the hem and smeared with mud.

She didn't look back.

She couldn't look back.

Looking back meant slowing down. Slowing down meant—

A howl split the air behind her.

Not a normal howl. Not the kind that came from a regular wolf, the kind you heard in nature documentaries or read about in storybooks.

This was wrong.

This was three voices layered into one—a horrible, dissonant harmony that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere all at once. It crawled down her spine like ice water and settled in her stomach like a stone.

She ran faster.

The trees blurred past her. Oak. Birch. Something with thorns that caught her sleeve and tore it. She wrenched free without slowing, leaving a scrap of yellow fabric behind like a flag of surrender.

Behind her, the forest moved.

Not the trees—the trees were still. But something within the trees was moving. Something large. Something that didn't care about stealth anymore, because it knew—they knew—that their prey was tiring.

Branches snapped. Not the small, casual snaps of a deer passing through. These were cracks, loud as gunshots, as if something with immense weight and zero concern for subtlety was simply shouldering its way through the undergrowth.

The girl's foot caught on a root.

She stumbled, caught herself on a tree trunk, and kept going. Her palm was bleeding now, scraped raw against the bark, but she didn't notice. Adrenaline was a cruel mistress—it kept you moving long after your body should have given up, kept you running even when every part of you screamed to just stop.

A growl rolled through the forest behind her.

Low. Long. Hungry.

She veered left, ducking under a low-hanging branch, and nearly collided with a massive spiderweb. She twisted away, lost her footing on the uneven ground, and slid down a small slope on her back, dirt and leaves cascading around her.

At the bottom, she scrambled up again.

Keep going. Keep going. Don't stop. Don't—

Another howl.

Closer now.

Much closer.

She burst through a final wall of ferns and—

The clearing.

It was beautiful. A perfect circle of grass, ringed by wildflowers, bathed in unfiltered sunlight. A place that should have felt safe. Sacred, even.

But there was nowhere to hide.

No trees to climb. No rocks to duck behind. Just open ground and blue sky and the terrible, creeping realization that she had made a mistake.

She sprinted across the clearing anyway.

Her foot caught on something—a small log, half-hidden in the grass—and she went down hard. The air left her lungs in a whoosh, and for a moment, the world went white with pain.

She rolled onto her back, gasping, and tried to push herself up.

Her ankle screamed.

She looked down. Her foot was wedged in a small hole—a burrow, maybe, or just a gap in the earth—and when she tried to pull it free, pain shot up her leg like lightning.

No. No, no, no—

She yanked harder. The pain made her vision blur, but she kept pulling, kept twisting, kept fighting—

And then she heard it.

The sound of something entering the clearing.

She stopped moving.

Slowly, trembling, she looked up.

Three of them.

They emerged from the treeline like nightmares given flesh—massive, hulking shapes that seemed to swallow the sunlight around them. Their fur was a patchwork of grey and black, matted and mangy, hanging off their bodies in uneven clumps. Their eyes burned like coals, and their mouths—

Their mouths were too wide.

Too full of teeth.

And behind them, three tails apiece. Thick, muscular things that twitched and swayed with an almost hypnotic rhythm, like they had minds of their own.

The creatures stopped at the edge of the clearing.

They didn't charge.

They didn't need to.

They knew.

The girl's breath came in short, sharp gasps. Tears carved clean tracks through the dirt on her cheeks. She pulled at her foot again, desperation lending her strength, but it wouldn't budge.

The wolves—if they could be called that—began to circle.

Slowly. Patiently. Their claws clicked against the grass, and their growls rumbled like distant thunder. One of them licked its lips, a long, pink tongue dragging across teeth that had no business existing on any natural creature.

They were savoring this.

They had been hungry for a long time.

And now, finally, they were going to eat.

The girl's chest heaved. A sob escaped her throat—small, broken, hopeless. She pressed herself back against the ground, as if she could somehow sink into the earth and disappear.

"Please," she whispered.

The word hung in the air, fragile and useless.

The largest of the wolves—the one with fur the color of storm clouds and eyes like dying embers—stepped forward. Its tails lashed behind it, and its mouth stretched into something that might have been a smile.

It was enjoying this.

The girl squeezed her eyes shut.

And then—

The forest screamed.

The leader stepped forward.

Its claws sank into the soft earth, each step deliberate, unhurried. The other two fanned out behind it, their tails swaying in that hypnotic rhythm, their growls a low, continuous rumble that vibrated through the ground itself.

The girl couldn't move. Couldn't breathe. Couldn't even scream.

The lead wolf's shadow fell over her, and she could smell it now—rot and decay and something metallic, like old blood. Its jaws parted, strings of saliva stretching between teeth that were far too long, far too sharp.

It lowered its head.

And then—

A growl.

But not from the wolf.

This growl came from behind the trees. From within the forest. It was deeper than the others—so deep it was almost felt rather than heard, a vibration that settled into the bones and rattled the teeth. It was louder, too. Much louder. And it was angry in a way that made the three wolves' hunger seem almost polite by comparison.

The lead wolf froze.

Its head snapped up, ears flattening against its skull. The other two stopped their circling, their growls dying in their throats as they turned toward the sound.

For a long moment, nothing moved.

The girl lay frozen on the ground, her heart pounding so hard she could feel it in her throat. She didn't understand what was happening. She didn't care. All she knew was that the monsters had stopped looking at her.

Then the forest exploded.

Two trees—full-grown oaks, their trunks as thick as a man's body—went flying sideways as if they weighed nothing. They crashed down on either side of the clearing, roots tearing free from the earth, leaves showering down like green rain.

And through the gap they left behind, something white came charging.

It was like them. But it wasn't.

Where the three wolves were mangy and grey, this creature was pure white—so white it seemed to glow in the sunlight, as if it had been carved from fresh snow or moonlight or something equally impossible. It was larger, too. Taller at the shoulder, broader in the chest, its muscles shifting beneath its fur like coiled steel.

And its tails.

Four of them.

They streamed behind it like banners as it ran, and around its body—rippling, flickering, alive—was a mist. A faint, shimmering energy that clung to its fur like morning fog and seemed to pulse with every beat of its heart.

The white wolf launched itself into the clearing and landed between the girl and the three monsters.

The ground shook.

The girl stared up at it, her mind blank with terror and confusion and something that might have been hope. The creature's fur was so close she could have reached out and touched it. The mist—that strange, glowing mist—drifted toward her, warm and soft, and for a moment, the pain in her ankle faded.

The three wolves recoiled.

Not much. Just a step. But it was enough.

The lead wolf recovered first. It was bigger than the others—scarred, battle-worn, with one ear that had been torn nearly in half. It studied the white wolf with eyes that held something more than hunger now.

Calculation.

It took a step forward. Then another. Its head lowered, and a growl rumbled from its chest—not a threat this time, but a question. A challenge.

Who are you?

The white wolf didn't growl back.

It didn't need to.

It simply stood there, four tails raised, energy mist crackling around its body like heat lightning. Its eyes—golden, bright as twin suns—locked onto the lead wolf with an intensity that made the air itself feel heavier.

The lead wolf hesitated.

Its paw hovered above the ground, trembling.

Then it lunged.

The fight was brief.

And brutal.

The lead wolf's teeth sank into the white wolf's shoulder—or tried to. The white wolf twisted, faster than something that size had any right to move, and its own jaws closed around the lead wolf's neck. Not hard enough to kill. Just hard enough to hurt.

The lead wolf yelped.

The other two charged in, snapping and snarling, their three tails lashing wildly. One went for the white wolf's flank. The other aimed for its hind legs.

The white wolf released the leader and spun, catching the second attacker mid-leap. Its jaws closed around the smaller wolf's foreleg, and with a single, contemptuous shake of its head, it sent the creature tumbling across the clearing like a ragdoll.

The third attacker faltered.

That was all the opening the white wolf needed.

It surged forward, slamming into the lead wolf with the force of a landslide. The two of them crashed into a tree at the edge of the clearing—through it, actually, the trunk splintering and cracking as they went—and when the dust cleared, the lead wolf was pinned beneath the white wolf's massive paws, its breath coming in ragged, wheezing gasps.

The white wolf leaned down.

Its muzzle was inches from the lead wolf's ear, and it made a sound—not a growl, but something softer. Something almost like a whisper.

Whatever it said, the lead wolf understood.

The white wolf stepped back.

The lead wolf scrambled to its feet, stumbling, favoring its left side. It looked at the white wolf—really looked—and for the first time, the hunger in its eyes was gone.

Replaced by something else.

Defeat.

It turned and fled.

The other two followed, limping and whimpering, disappearing into the trees without a single backward glance. The sound of their retreat faded quickly, swallowed by the forest, until there was nothing left but the rustle of leaves and the distant call of birds.

The white wolf stood motionless for a long moment, watching them go.

Then it turned.

The girl was still on the ground, her foot still wedged in the hole, her body trembling with exhaustion and fear. She should have been terrified. She was terrified. But as the white wolf padded toward her, the energy mist around it intensified—brightening, swirling, pulsing with light so bright she had to squint.

And then it changed.

The transformation was instantaneous. One moment, a massive wolf stood before her. The next, there was a woman.

She was tall—taller than any woman the girl had ever seen—with hair the color of fresh snow that fell past her waist in a silken cascade. Her eyes were the same golden hue as the wolf's had been, warm and sharp and impossibly old. She wore simple clothing—a tunic of soft grey, trousers that hugged her legs, boots that seemed to have been made from the same white fur as her wolf form.

She was beautiful.

And terrifying.

And when she knelt beside the girl, her expression was... gentle.

"You're hurt," the woman said. Her voice was low and melodic, like wind through autumn leaves. She reached for the girl's leg, her fingers cool against the feverish skin. "Let me see."

The girl flinched.

The woman paused. Her golden eyes met the girl's, and something flickered in their depths—understanding, maybe. Or pity.

"I'm not going to hurt you," she said quietly. "I promise."

She examined the girl's ankle with careful, practiced hands. Her touch was light, but the girl still hissed through her teeth when the woman's fingers probed a particularly tender spot.

"It's not broken," the woman murmured, more to herself than to the girl. "Just sprained. And you've got some cuts that need cleaning, but nothing serious."

She gripped the girl's calf with one hand and the top of her foot with the other, then gently—so gently—worked the foot free of the hole. The girl bit her lip to keep from crying out, but the pain was brief, and when it was over, the woman sat back on her heels and studied her.

The girl stared back.

Her mind was still spinning, still trying to catch up with everything that had happened. The monsters. The chase. The white wolf. The woman who had appeared out of nowhere.

She should have been running.

She should have been screaming.

But instead, she just sat there, frozen, her heart pounding in her chest.

The woman's expression softened. She reached out—slowly, giving the girl time to pull away—and brushed a strand of hair from her face.

"You're okay," she said.

And then, after a moment—

"You okay, Neve?"

The girl's breath caught.

Her name.

The woman had said her name.

Neve blinked, her mouth opening and closing soundlessly. She wanted to ask how the woman knew her name. She wanted to ask what was happening. She wanted to ask a hundred questions, a thousand questions, but none of them would come out.

So instead, she just nodded.

And the woman smiled.

It was a small thing—just a slight curve of her lips—but it was warm, and real, and it made Neve feel, for the first time since she'd started running, that maybe—just maybe—everything was going to be all right.

The woman's smile lingered for a moment, then faded into something softer. She sat back on her heels, giving Neve space, and glanced around the clearing as if checking for any lingering threats.

The forest was quiet now. Peaceful, even.

You'd never know that minutes ago, three monsters had been circling a terrified little girl.

"We should get you home," the woman said, turning back to Neve. "Can you stand?"

Neve nodded, though she wasn't sure it was true. She pushed herself up on shaky arms, and the woman rose with her, one hand hovering near Neve's elbow in case she fell.

She didn't fall. Her ankle throbbed, and her whole body ached, and her dress was ruined, but she was standing.

The woman looked at her for a long moment.

There was something in her eyes—something searching, almost sad. Like she was looking at a puzzle she'd been trying to solve for a very long time.

Then she blinked, and the look was gone.

"Come on," she said, and she started walking toward the treeline.

Neve hesitated.

The forest loomed ahead of her, dark and deep and full of things she didn't understand. The memory of the chase was still fresh—the howls, the snapping branches, the terror that had driven her feet forward long after her lungs had begged her to stop.

But the woman had stopped walking. She was looking back over her shoulder, waiting.

And she had saved Neve.

And she knew Neve's name.

Neve didn't ask how. She didn't ask anything. She just limped forward, one painful step at a time, and followed the white-haired woman into the trees.

The woman didn't speak as they walked.

She didn't need to. Somehow, her presence was enough—a quiet, steady comfort that seemed to push back the shadows and the fear. The birds had started singing again. The sunlight still filtered through the leaves in golden shafts.

It was beautiful.

It was safe.

Neve stole glances at the woman as they walked. She was so tall. So graceful. Her white hair swayed gently as she moved, and her golden eyes were always looking ahead, always watching.

She looked... familiar.

Not her face. Neve had never seen this woman before in her life. She would have remembered.

But something about her. The way she moved. The way she held her shoulders. The way her lips curved when she smiled.

Neve's chest ached.

She didn't know why.

They walked for a long time. Longer than Neve thought she could walk. Her ankle screamed with every step, but she didn't complain. She just kept going, one foot in front of the other, her eyes fixed on the woman's back.

Eventually, the trees began to thin.

Neve saw it first—a flash of color through the branches. Red. Orange. Yellow.

Apples.

The orchard.

Her heart leaped.

She knew this place. She knew these trees. She'd climbed them a hundred times, scraped her knees on their branches, stuffed her pockets with fruit until they bulged.

This was her orchard.

Which meant—

The house appeared.

Small. Wooden. With a stone chimney and a crooked porch and a garden that had seen better days. Smoke curled from the chimney, thin and grey against the blue sky.

Home.

Neve stopped walking.

The woman stopped too. She turned, and for a moment, her golden eyes held something Neve couldn't name.

"You're safe now," the woman said.

Neve looked at her.

Looked at the house.

Looked back at the woman.

She should have run to the door. Should have burst inside and thrown herself into her mother's arms and cried until she had no tears left. That's what a normal girl would do.

But Neve wasn't normal.

She hadn't been normal for a long time.

And the woman—the wolf—the stranger who knew her name—was still standing there, waiting.

Not for thanks.

Not for anything.

Just... waiting.

Like she had all the time in the world.

Like she'd been waiting a very, very long time already.

Neve opened her mouth.

Closed it.

Then, without a word, she limped forward and wrapped her arms around the woman's waist.

The woman went still.

For a heartbeat, Neve thought she'd made a mistake. Thought the woman would push her away, would vanish into mist and memory, would leave her standing alone in the orchard with nothing but questions.

But then—

The woman's arms came up.

Slowly. Carefully. Like she was holding something precious. Something fragile.

Something she'd been missing for a very long time.

She didn't speak.

Neither did Neve.

They just stood there, in the shadow of the apple trees, holding each other while the sun climbed higher and the smoke rose from the chimney and somewhere inside the house, a woman called out a name.

"Neve?"

Neve pulled back.

The woman let her go.

Their eyes met—golden and brown, ancient and young—and for just a moment, Neve saw something in the woman's face. Something raw. Something aching.

Something like goodbye.

"Go on," the woman whispered.

Neve didn't move.

"Go on," the woman said again, and this time her voice was firmer. "Your mother's waiting."

Neve's throat tightened.

She wanted to ask. She wanted to know.

But the woman was already turning away. Already walking back toward the trees, her white hair catching the sunlight like spun glass.

"Wait—" Neve started.

The woman paused.

She didn't turn around.

"You'll see me again," she said. "I promise."

And then she was gone.

Not into mist. Not in a flash of light. She just... walked into the trees, and the shadows swallowed her, and Neve was alone.

Standing in the orchard.

Standing in the sunlight.

Standing in front of her house, with her ankle throbbing and her dress torn and her heart full of something she couldn't name.

"Neve!"

Her mother's voice again. Closer now. Frantic.

Neve turned toward the house.

The door burst open, and her mother came running, her apron still on, her hands covered in flour, her face pale with worry.

She swept Neve into her arms.

"Oh, thank God," her mother breathed. "Oh, thank God, thank God—"

Neve let herself be held.

She let herself be carried inside.

She let herself be fussed over and bandaged and fed and tucked into bed.

And when her mother finally left the room, when the door clicked shut and the house fell quiet, Neve stared at the ceiling and thought about golden eyes and white hair and a name spoken by a stranger who wasn't a stranger.

You'll see me again.

She closed her eyes.

I promise.

Outside, in the trees at the edge of the orchard, the white wolf sat perfectly still.

The lights of the small wooden house flickered through the branches—warm and yellow and alive. She could hear the faint sounds of movement inside. A mother's footsteps. A kettle boiling. The creak of a bed frame as a little girl shifted beneath her blankets.

She didn't move.

She didn't sleep.

She just watched.

And waited.

The four tails curled around her body, and the energy mist that clung to her fur had dimmed to a faint, pulsing glow—like embers after a fire has burned itself out.

She would stay here tonight.

And tomorrow night.

And the night after that.

For as long as it took.

Because she had made a promise once—a long time ago—and she intended to keep it.

No matter how much it hurt.

No matter how much she wanted to walk up to that door and knock and see the look on her mother's face.

No matter how much she wanted to hold her little sister and never let go.

She stayed in the shadows.

She watched the house.

And she waited.
