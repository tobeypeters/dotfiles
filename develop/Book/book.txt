# Chapter One: The White Guardian

The forest glimmered beneath the morning sun. Golden light filtered through the canopy, dappling the moss and wildflowers. Birds called overhead, and the air smelled of damp earth and something ancient.

Through that quiet beauty, bare feet pounded the ground—small, dirty feet that had run so long they barely felt the stones and roots cutting into them.

The girl crashed through a curtain of hanging vines, her breath coming in ragged gasps. Her hair—what had once been a neat braid—had come loose and wild, tangled with leaves and sweat. Her dress, a simple cotton thing that might have been yellow once, was torn at the hem and smeared with mud.

She didn't look back.

She couldn't look back.

Looking back meant slowing down. Slowing down meant—

A howl split the air behind her.

It wasn't an ordinary wolf's howl. Three voices layered into one, a dissonant harmony that seemed to come from everywhere at once. It crawled down her spine like ice water and settled in her stomach like a stone.

She ran faster.

The trees blurred past her. Oak. Birch. Something with thorns that caught her sleeve and tore it. She wrenched free without slowing, leaving a scrap of yellow fabric behind like a flag of surrender.

Behind her, something large moved through the forest. It no longer cared about stealth. It knew its prey was tiring.

Branches cracked like gunshots as the creature shouldered through the undergrowth.

Her foot caught on a root. She stumbled, scraped her palm against a tree trunk, and kept running. Blood streaked her hand, but she barely noticed. Fear drove her onward long after her body begged her to stop.

A growl rolled through the forest behind her.

Low. Long. Hungry.

She veered left, ducking under a low-hanging branch, and nearly collided with a massive spiderweb. She twisted away, lost her footing on the uneven ground, and slid down a small slope on her back, dirt and leaves cascading around her.

At the bottom, she scrambled up again.

Keep going. Keep going. Don't stop. Don't—

Another howl.

Closer now.

Much closer.

She burst through a final wall of ferns and—

The clearing.

A perfect circle of grass lay ringed with violet, gold, and pale-pink wildflowers. Sunlight pooled on the clearing like honey, and tiny blue butterflies drifted through it, their wings flashing like fragments of stained glass. It should have felt peaceful.

But there was nowhere to hide.

No trees to climb. No rocks to duck behind. Just open ground and blue sky and the terrible, creeping realization that she had made a mistake.

She sprinted across the clearing anyway.

Her foot caught on something—a shallow hollow, half-hidden in the grass—and she went down hard. The air left her lungs in a whoosh, and for a moment, the world went white with pain.

She rolled onto her back, gasping, and tried to push herself up.

Her ankle screamed.

She looked down. Her foot was wedged in the hollow—a burrow, maybe, or just a gap in the earth—and when she tried to pull it free, pain shot up her leg like lightning.

No. No, no, no—

She yanked harder. The pain made her vision blur, but she kept pulling, kept twisting, kept fighting—

And then she heard it.

The sound of something entering the clearing.

She stopped moving.

Slowly, trembling, she looked up.

Three creatures emerged from the treeline, hulking shapes that seemed to swallow the sunlight. Their grey-black fur hung in matted clumps, their eyes burned like coals, and their mouths were unnaturally wide, crowded with teeth. Each had three thick tails that twitched with a life of their own.

The creatures stopped at the edge of the clearing.

They didn't charge.

They didn't need to.

They knew.

The girl's breath came in sharp gasps. Tears cut tracks through the dirt on her cheeks. She pulled at her trapped foot again, but it wouldn't budge.

The wolves, if they could be called that, began to circle. Their claws clicked against the grass, and their growls rumbled like distant thunder. One licked its lips, revealing teeth no natural creature should possess.

They had been hungry for a long time. At last, they had found their prey.

The girl's chest heaved. A sob escaped her throat—small, broken, hopeless. She pressed herself back against the ground, as if she could somehow sink into the earth and disappear.

Then she raised her arm.

Her hand was shaking—a fine, uncontrollable tremor that ran from her fingertips to her elbow. She pointed her palm at the wolves, her jaw clenched tight, her brow furrowed with desperate concentration.

For a moment, nothing happened.

Then—a flicker. A tiny burst of light, pale and blue-white, crackling off her fingertips like sparks from a dying fire. They danced in the air for half a heartbeat, bright and fragile, before fizzling into nothing.

The wolves didn't even flinch.

The lead wolf tilted its head, watching the display with what looked almost like amusement. Its tails swayed behind it.

"Please," she whispered.

The word hung in the air.

The girl's arm trembled harder. She tried again—squeezed her eyes shut, focused everything she had left into her palm, into her fingers, into the spark that refused to catch—

Nothing.

Her arm dropped to her side, limp and useless.

The largest of the wolves—the one with fur the color of storm clouds and eyes like dying embers—stepped forward. Its tails lashed behind it, and its mouth stretched into something that might have been a smile.

She squeezed her eyes shut as the leader advanced, claws sinking into the earth. The other two fanned out behind it, their tails swaying while their growls vibrated through the ground.

The girl couldn't move or breathe, couldn't even force a scream from her throat.

The lead wolf's shadow fell over her, and she could smell it now—rot and decay and something metallic, like old blood. Its jaws parted, strings of saliva stretching between teeth that were far too long, far too sharp.

It lowered its head.

And then—

The air itself seemed to shatter.

A growl rolled from the forest behind the trees—not from any of the three wolves. Deeper than their snarls, it settled into bone and rattled teeth. There was anger in it so immense that the wolves' hunger seemed almost polite by comparison.

The lead wolf froze.

Its head snapped up, ears flattening against its skull. The other two stopped their circling, their growls dying in their throats as they turned toward the sound.

For a long moment, nothing moved.

Then the forest exploded.

Two trees—full-grown oaks, their trunks as thick as a man's body—went flying sideways as if they weighed nothing. They crashed down on either side of the clearing, roots tearing free from the earth, leaves showering down like green rain.

And through the gap they left behind, something white came charging.

It was like them. But it wasn't.

Unlike the mangy grey wolves, this creature was pure white, its fur bright as fresh snow. It stood taller and broader than its rivals, muscles shifting beneath its coat like coiled steel. Four tails streamed behind it, surrounded by a shimmering mist that pulsed with every beat of its heart.

The white wolf launched itself into the clearing and landed between the girl and the three wolves.

The ground shook.

The girl felt the terror leave her. Her shoulders lowered, her breathing steadied, and the tears on her cheeks caught the sunlight.

She was safe. She couldn't have explained how she knew, but the certainty settled over her like a warm blanket, and she let herself believe it.

All three wolves recoiled.

Not much. Just a step. But it was enough.

The lead wolf recovered first. Larger than the others, scarred and battle-worn, with one ear torn nearly in half, it studied the white wolf with something beyond hunger in its eyes: calculation.

It took a step forward. Then another. Its head lowered, and a growl rumbled from its chest—not a threat this time, but a question. A challenge.

The white wolf didn't growl back.

It didn't need to.

It simply stood there, four tails raised, energy mist crackling around its body like heat lightning. Its eyes—golden, bright as twin suns—locked onto the lead wolf with an intensity that made the air itself feel heavier.

The lead wolf hesitated, one paw hovering above the ground. Then it lunged.

The fight was brief and brutal. The lead wolf lunged for the white wolf's shoulder, but the larger creature twisted with startling speed and caught it by the neck—not hard enough to kill, but hard enough to hurt.

The lead wolf yelped.

The other two charged in, snapping and snarling, their three tails lashing wildly. One went for the white wolf's flank. The other aimed for its hind legs.

The white wolf released the leader and spun, catching the second attacker mid-leap. Its jaws closed around the smaller wolf's foreleg, and one sharp shake sent it tumbling across the clearing.

The third attacker faltered, and the white wolf seized the opening.

It surged forward, slamming into the lead wolf with the force of a landslide. The two of them crashed into a tree at the edge of the clearing—through it, actually, the trunk splintering and cracking as they went—and when the dust cleared, the lead wolf was pinned beneath the white wolf's massive paws, its breath coming in ragged, wheezing gasps.

The white wolf leaned down until its muzzle was inches from the lead wolf's ear. It made a sound softer than a growl, a low, rolling murmur that sounded almost like words: a warning, and a promise.

The lead wolf understood.

The white wolf stepped back.

The lead wolf scrambled to its feet, stumbling, favoring its left side. It looked at the white wolf—really looked—and for the first time, the hunger in its eyes was gone.

Replaced by something else.

Defeat.

It turned and fled.

The other two followed, limping and whimpering, disappearing into the trees without a single backward glance. The sound of their retreat faded quickly, swallowed by the forest, until there was nothing left but the rustle of leaves and the distant call of birds.

The white wolf watched them disappear before turning back.

The girl was still on the ground, her foot still wedged in the hollow, but she wasn't trembling anymore. She was looking up at the white wolf with wide eyes and a small, shaky smile.

The white wolf padded toward her, and as it moved, the energy mist around it intensified—brightening, swirling, pulsing with light so bright the girl had to squint.

And then it changed.

The transformation was smooth—fluid, like water flowing into a new shape. One moment, a massive wolf stood before her. The next, there was a young woman.

She was tall, with hair the color of autumn leaves—deep red, cascading past her shoulders in gentle waves. As the last wisps of energy mist curled off her, they carried with them a faint, sweet scent. Strawberries—wild and sun-ripened, the kind that grew in hidden patches deep in the forest. The smell lingered in the air around her, soft and unexpected, like a memory of summer. Her eyes, which had been golden and burning just moments before, softened and shifted, the last traces of that strange energy bleeding away until they were simply green. A beautiful, warm green, like sunlight through leaves. Her clothing was simple—a tunic of soft grey, trousers that hugged her legs, boots that seemed made for long walks.

The last wisps of energy mist curled off her shoulders, off her arms, off the tips of her hair, and then faded into nothing.

She was just a young woman now.

A familiar young woman.

"Rayne," the girl breathed.

Rayne knelt beside her sister, her green eyes scanning Neve's face, her body, the awkward angle of her leg. Her expression was a mix of relief and worry and something else—something that looked a lot like exasperation.

But beneath the relief, beneath the practiced calm of her hands, a question gnawed at her.

Why were they here?

The grey wolves—the three-tailed ones, the ones that hunted in packs and left nothing but bones behind—they belonged to the far north. The frozen reaches. The pine forests and the snow-choked valleys that bordered the kingdom's edge. They had never, not once in all the years Rayne had patrolled these lands, been seen this far south. They didn't migrate. They didn't wander. They stayed where the cold was, where the prey was slow and fat and easy.

So why?

Why were they here, in the heart of the southern wood, chasing a child through a clearing that should have been safe?

The thought settled in her stomach like a stone. Something was wrong. Something had changed—or was changing—and she didn't know what.

She filed it away. Later. She would think about it later.

Right now, her sister needed her.

"You're hurt," she said. Her voice was low and melodic, but there was a firm edge to it. "Let me see."

Neve flinched as Rayne's fingers found her ankle. But Rayne's touch was gentle, practiced. She probed carefully, her brow furrowing.

"It's not broken," she murmured after a moment. "Just sprained. And you've got some cuts that need cleaning, but nothing serious."

She sat back on her heels, and for just a moment, her hands trembled. Then she pressed them flat against her thighs and looked at her sister.

"You okay, Neve?"

Neve nodded, a small smile breaking through the dirt and tears on her face.

"Yes, sister. I'm alright."

Rayne exhaled—slowly, deliberately. "Good." She paused. "Because I'm going to kill you myself if you ever do that again."

Neve's smile flickered, caught somewhere between relief and guilt.

Rayne gripped Neve's calf with one hand and the top of her foot with the other, then carefully—so carefully—worked the foot free of the hollow. Neve bit her lip to keep from crying out, but the pain was brief.

When it was over, Rayne sat back and looked at her sister. Her expression softened for just a moment. Then it hardened again—not angry, exactly, but concerned. The way an older sibling gets when they've been scared half to death and are trying very hard not to show it.

"We've been looking for you for hours," Rayne said. "Father's furious. Or he will be, once he stops being terrified. At present, I suspect he's managing both at once."

Neve winced. "I know. I just—"

"Today of all days, Neve. The envoys from the eastern court arrive at sundown. You knew that."

Neve looked down at her hands, fidgeting with the torn hem of her dress.

"I know, I just—" She swallowed. "The wildflowers. Down by the falls. The guards said they were blooming, and I've never seen them, and I thought if I was quick—" She stopped. "I thought I'd be back before anyone noticed."

Rayne sighed.

"And before you could get there, the wolves started chasing you."

Neve nodded.

Rayne was quiet for a moment. Then she reached out and brushed a strand of hair from Neve's face, tucking it behind her ear.

"All right," she said. "We'd better get you home before Father sends out a search party large enough to qualify as an invasion."

Neve looked up at her, eyes wide.

"But Father—"

"I'll talk to Father for you," Rayne said. "Try to smooth things over."

Neve's face lit up. She threw her arms around Rayne's neck, hugging her tight.

"Thank you, Rayne," she whispered. "I love you."

Rayne hesitated for just a heartbeat.

Then her arms came up, wrapping around her little sister, holding her close.

"I love you too," she said softly.

She held on for a moment longer than necessary—like she was afraid Neve might disappear if she let go. Then she pulled back, her green eyes bright.

"Let's go."

She stood, then reached down and helped Neve to her feet. Neve wobbled, her ankle protesting, and Rayne pulled her close—not just for support, but for a moment of warmth. Then she adjusted her grip, one arm around her sister's shoulders, taking most of her weight.

Together, the two sisters started walking.

Out of the clearing.

Through the trees.

Toward home.

And behind them, the forest settled back into its quiet rhythm, as if nothing had happened at all.

# Chapter Two: Flame is Lit

The town of Verdale was already wide awake by the time the sun climbed high over the eastern hills.

It was the largest town in the kingdom—not by half measures, but by every measure that mattered. Larger than any other settlement in the realm. And it showed in everything. In the breadth of its streets, wide enough for a dozen carts to pass abreast. In the height of its buildings, three and four stories tall, their timber frames dark with age and their plaster walls freshly whitewashed. In the sheer, bustling, impossible energy of the place.

By late morning, the town was alive.

The bakers had been up since before dawn, their ovens glowing orange in the blue-dark hours, their bread already sold and eaten and replaced by fresh loaves that steamed on their shelves. The merchants had opened their shutters hours ago, their wares arranged with practiced hands. Ribbons and ribbons of silk in every color. Spices in little wooden bowls, their scents sharp and exotic. Leather goods. Pottery. Ironwork. Apples stacked in perfect pyramids, their skins still beaded with morning dew. Fish carts, their silver scales glittering. Flower stands, their blooms spilling over in red and gold and violet.

And the people. Everywhere, the people.

Verdale was a town that never truly slept, but late morning was when it hit its stride. The streets were thronged with bodies—merchants and farmers, travelers and townsfolk, children and elders and everyone in between. They moved in a great, churning river of humanity, flowing between the stalls and shops, their voices rising in a constant, cheerful roar.

A blacksmith's hammer rang out in steady rhythm. A seamstress sat in her window, her needle flashing in the sunlight as she worked on a gown of deep blue velvet. A juggler—young and nimble-fingered—tossed five brightly painted balls in a lazy circle while a small crowd applauded and tossed coins into the cap at his feet. A group of children gathered around a puppeteer, their faces upturned in wonder as a tiny wooden dragon swooped and dove above their heads. An old woman with a face like a wrinkled apple called out her prices in a voice worn smooth by decades of haggling.

The town smelled of bread and smoke and flowers and horses, and beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, was the salt tang of the sea. It sounded like laughter and haggling and the clatter of cartwheels on stone, and beneath it all, the distant cry of gulls. It felt like a place where nothing bad had ever happened, and nothing bad ever would.

The main road ran through the heart of it all, and if you followed it—past the blacksmith's forge, past the seamstress's shop, past the juggler and the puppeteer and the old woman with her apples, past a cart piled high with fresh fish, past a group of merchants arguing good-naturedly over the price of wool, past a young couple stealing a kiss in the shadow of an awning, past a dog chasing a cat chasing a pigeon—you would eventually reach the place where the street opened, and the sea revealed itself.

The port of Verdale was a world unto its own—a great sprawling crescent of stone docks and wooden piers that curved along the coastline like a smile. The water in the harbor was deep and blue and restless, chop-tipped with white where the wind caught it, and it was crowded with ships. Merchant vessels with their sails furled, their decks piled high with crates and barrels and bolts of cloth. Private boats, sleek and elegant, their polished hulls gleaming with varnish and brass. Fishing boats, sturdy and weathered, their nets spread out to dry on the docks. And everywhere, everywhere, boats were coming and going—oars dipping, sails snapping, hulls knocking gently against the piers as they docked and departed.

The docks themselves were a hive of motion. Dock workers hauled crates and rolled barrels and shouted to one another in a dozen different dialects. Sailors mended nets and coiled ropes and argued about the weather. Merchants haggled with ship captains over the price of cargo. Gulls wheeled overhead, screaming their raucous cries, diving for scraps. The smell of salt and fish and tar and rope hung thick in the air, and beneath it all was the steady rhythm of the sea—waves slapping against stone, ropes creaking, the distant call of a horn from some ship preparing to depart.

And beyond the port, beyond the docks and the boats and the churning blue water, the hill rose.

It was a large hill—broad and green and rounded, its slopes thick with forest, its crown a bare outcropping of pale grey stone. Not a mountain, not quite. Something gentler than that. Something that had been shaped by centuries of wind and rain and the slow, patient work of the sea. And there, perched atop it, the castle sat—its towers and walls and pennants catching the late morning light, its pale stone glowing against the blue of the sky. The sea flanked it on two sides, a vast expanse of glittering water that stretched to the horizon, its waves breaking white against the rocky base of the hill far below.

The main road wound up the hillside in a series of gentle switchbacks, its surface paved with smooth grey stone, its edges lined with low walls of fitted rock. Trees grew thick on either side—oak and pine and something with silver leaves that rustled softly in the sea breeze. The air grew cooler as the road climbed, and the cries of gulls grew louder, and the smell of salt grew stronger.

And then, at last, the road leveled out—and there, before the castle's front gate, spanning a deep chasm that had been carved into the hill itself, was the drawbridge.

It was enormous.

Wide enough, certainly, for four or five horse-drawn wagons to cross side by side without ever touching. Long enough that eight wagons could have lined up nose-to-tail along its length with room to spare. The planks were thick oak, banded with iron, and they rang softly under the hooves of the occasional rider or the wheels of a merchant's cart. Iron chains, each link as thick as a man's forearm, connected the bridge to the gatehouse, ready to raise it at a moment's notice.

But no one was raising it today.

Today, the drawbridge was down. Today, the gates stood open. Today, the castle was expecting company.

Crossing the bridge, passing through the shadow of the gatehouse, you came into the courtyard.

And it was massive.

A sprawling expanse of swept stone, so wide it seemed to swallow the light. To the left, the guard quarters—long, low buildings of pale grey stone, their doors standing open to let in the morning air. Beyond them, a mess hall, its chimney already smoking, the smell of something savory drifting out on the breeze. And scattered among them, smaller buildings—offices, perhaps, or storerooms, their purposes known only to those who worked within.

To the right, the stables—a long row of stalls, their doors open, horses stamping and nickering in the shadows. A blacksmith's forge stood nearby, its hammer ringing out in steady rhythm, sparks flying from the anvil. Beyond it, a weapons repository, its doors guarded by two soldiers in crimson tabards. And further still, training rings—circular patches of packed earth, surrounded by low stone walls, where soldiers drilled and sparred and shouted in the morning sun.

And down by the sea, two ports.

The first was for the military fleet—its warships sleek and dark and bristling with iron, their hulls low in the water, their decks crowded with ballistae and catapults and rows of shielded soldiers. They sat at anchor in neat, orderly lines, their banners snapping in the wind, their crews moving across their decks with the practiced efficiency of long familiarity.

The second port was further down, adjacent to the castle itself, and it was there that the royal and special guest ships docked. And there, in the finest berth of all, sat a ship unlike any other in the harbor.

It was beautiful.

White as seafoam, its hull gleaming with fresh varnish, its decks polished to a shine. Its sails were furled, but even furled they were magnificent—white and blue, the fabric so fine it seemed to shimmer in the sunlight. And on those sails, worked in shades of blue that deepened and lightened with every fold of the cloth, was a wind drake—its wings spread wide, its tail curling, its eyes bright and fierce and alive. The ship itself was large, easily the size of a merchant vessel, but there was a grace to its lines, a kind of elegant power, that made it look almost as if it had been built for a king.

Which, of course, it had.

But beneath that beautiful white hull, hidden from casual view, was something else entirely. Iron plating, thick and dark, bolted to the frame beneath the waterline. Ballistae, folded and concealed beneath polished decking. A ram, heavy and sharp, tucked beneath the prow like a secret. The ship was a warship—a battle-ready vessel disguised as a luxury yacht, its beauty a mask for its true purpose.

And out in the water, at a close but decent distance, similar ships sat at anchor. Their sails were furled, their decks quiet, but there was no mistaking what they were. They watched the harbor the way a hawk watches a field—patient, still, and utterly, completely ready.

Back in the courtyard, in the center of it all, stood a fountain.

It was a dragon.

Not a monster, not a beast of nightmare—but something older, something quieter. A dragon of the earth element, carved from stone the color of moss and riverbed, its body low and powerful, its wings folded against its back, its tail curled around the base of the fountain like a cat's. Its head was raised, its jaws open, and from its mouth poured a steady stream of water—clear and bright, catching the sunlight and scattering it into a thousand tiny rainbows. The water fell into a wide basin below, its surface rippling and gleaming, and around the rim of the basin, carved in letters worn smooth by time, were words in a language no one remembered anymore.

Behind the fountain, the ground rose.

Broad, beautiful stairs swept upward, their steps wide and shallow, carved from the same pale grey stone as the castle walls. They climbed in a gentle curve, flanked on either side by low walls of fitted rock, their surfaces worn smooth by centuries of footsteps. At the top of the stairs, the castle itself waited—tall and proud, its towers rising against the blue of the sky, its windows glittering in the sunlight.

And at the top of the stairs, set into the castle's front wall, was a door.

It was massive.

Deep, rich wood, dark as old honey, its surface polished to a gleam that caught the light and held it. Iron bands crossed it in wide, sturdy stripes, their edges worked with patterns too subtle to see from a distance. And in the center of the door, where the two halves met, a dragon's head protruded—carved from the same dark wood, its features sharp and fierce, its eyes closed, its jaws slightly parted. When the doors were closed, the dragon's head was whole, a single creature watching over the castle's threshold. When the doors opened, it would split in half—two halves of the same face, drawing apart to let the world inside.

The doors swung open, and the castle swallowed them whole.

Inside, the air was cool and still, carrying the faint scent of stone and candlewax and something older—something that had been part of these walls for so long it had seeped into the mortar itself. The entry hall was wide and high, its ceiling lost in shadow, its floor a mosaic of pale tiles that depicted, in faded colors, a great serpent coiled around a mountain.

But it was only a passage.

Beyond it, through another set of doors—these thrown wide, their hinges singing softly—lay the throne room.

It was enormous.

Two levels of it, one stacked above the other. The lower level was a vast expanse of polished stone, its floor so smooth it reflected the light from above like a dark mirror. The upper level was a walkway that ringed the entire room, its balustrade carved from pale marble, its arches opening onto shadowed corridors that led deeper into the castle. Guards stood along the walkway at intervals—their crimson tabards bright against the grey stone, their spears held upright, their eyes fixed on the room below.

And at the far end of the hall, behind the throne, were the windows.

They were massive. Stained glass, worked in a thousand colors, rising from the floor almost to the ceiling. The light poured through them in great shafts of gold and crimson and blue, scattering across the polished floor, painting the room in shifting, jewel-toned light. The images in the glass were old—so old that no one remembered who had made them, or why. A dragon, its wings spread wide. A mountain, its peak crowned with fire. A river, winding through a forest of silver trees. And above it all, a sky full of stars, their light captured in glass and held there forever.

Before the windows, on a dais of pale stone, stood the throne.

It was a massive thing, carved from dark wood and banded with iron, its back rising high above the head of anyone who sat in it. The arms were shaped like dragon claws, their talons curling around the ends of the armrests. The legs were carved to look like scaled feet, their claws gripping the stone of the dais. And above the throne, worked into the wood itself, was a dragon's head—its jaws open, its eyes bright with something that might have been gold, or might have been fire.

On either side of the throne, slightly lower, slightly smaller, were two other chairs. They were elegant things, upholstered in deep crimson velvet, their frames worked with gold. Not thrones, exactly. But close.

The room was full of people.

They stood in groups, scattered across the polished floor like islands in a dark sea. Nobles in silks and velvets, their jewels catching the light. Merchants in more practical garb, their hands calloused, their eyes sharp. Soldiers in crimson and gold, their postures relaxed but their eyes never still. Servants moving among them with trays of wine and small, delicate pastries. The air was filled with the sound of conversation—laughter, the clink of glasses, the murmur of voices rising and falling like the tide.

Near the doors at the back of the room, a small group stood together.

Four of them. Three soldiers—two men and one woman—and Rayne.

The soldiers were large. Not just tall, but broad, their shoulders straining the fabric of their uniforms, their arms thick with muscle. The woman, Maren, was the tallest of them, her hair cropped short and dark, her face sharp and angular, her eyes bright with amusement. The men were brothers, or looked it—same broad faces, same wide grins, same easy way of standing, as if they'd never been uncomfortable in their lives. Garrick was the elder, his voice the loudest, his laugh the quickest. Corin was the younger, quieter, his eyes always watching, always measuring.

They were laughing.

Rayne stood among them, her red hair loose around her shoulders, her green eyes bright. She was young—barely into her thirties, though the confidence in her bearing made her seem older. She was smiling—really smiling, the kind that crinkled the corners of her eyes and made her look younger than she was. Garrick had just finished telling a story, and Rayne was shaking her head, her laugh ringing out clear and warm above the noise of the room.

"—and then he said, 'That's not a chicken, that's my wife!'" Garrick finished, his voice booming with delight.

Maren snorted. "You've told that story a hundred times. It wasn't funny the first time."

"It was hilarious the first time. You just have no sense of humor."

"I have plenty of sense of humor. I just don't laugh at the same joke for a decade."

Rayne grinned. "I thought it was funny."

"Thank you, Rayne. At least someone appreciates good comedy."

"Good comedy? That was barely comedy. That was a series of words arranged in the shape of a joke."

"That's philosophy, that is. Deep philosophy."

None of them noticed the doors at the back of the room swing open. None of them noticed the woman who stepped through them—young, barely into her twenties, her long blonde hair cascading past her shoulders, her blue clothing catching the light. None of them noticed her at all.

She walked straight down the carpet runner toward the throne.

And she didn't slow down.

Rayne was still standing near the center of the runner, her attention fixed on her friends, her laugh still fading from her lips. She didn't see the woman coming. Didn't step aside.

The woman slammed her shoulder into Rayne's as she passed—a hard, deliberate blow that sent Rayne staggering half a step sideways. The woman didn't break stride. Didn't falter. She kept walking, her chin high, her eyes fixed on the throne, as if Rayne wasn't even there.

Rayne caught her balance and turned, her green eyes blazing.

Hate. Pure, undiluted hate. It burned in her face, in her clenched jaw, in the way her hands curled into fists at her sides.

Corin stepped forward, his face dark with anger, his hand already reaching for his sword. "Rayne—"

Rayne threw her arm up. Her index finger pointed at the ceiling, sharp and absolute.

Corin stopped cold.

She didn't look at him. Didn't say a word. But the command was clear—stay. Don't move. Don't interfere.

Corin's jaw tightened. His hand fell back to his side.

Rayne turned and followed the woman.

She didn't run. Didn't hurry. Her steps were measured, deliberate, her back straight, her chin high. She followed the woman down the carpet runner, past the nobles and the merchants and the servants, past the guards along the walls, all the way to the dais, all the way to the throne.

The woman stopped before the throne, her back to the room, her eyes fixed on the stained glass windows.

Rayne pulled up behind her.

She didn't touch her. Didn't speak. She simply stood there, a pace behind, and cleared her throat—a sharp, deliberate sound that cut through the quiet of the room.

The woman turned.

Her blue eyes met Rayne's green, and for a moment, neither of them moved. The woman's lip curled—just slightly, just enough to show the edge of her teeth.

"What?" she snapped.

Rayne didn't answer.

She just stared. Her green eyes flat, her face expressionless, her arms loose at her sides. She stared at the woman the way a hawk stares at a field mouse—patient, still, and utterly, completely unimpressed.

The woman's glare flickered.

Then—something shifted. Her eyes widened, just slightly. Her head tilted, just a fraction. Recognition, maybe. Or realization. Or something else entirely.

"Oh," she said. "Here."

She reached up and unfastened her cloak—a deep blue thing, its fabric the same rich color as her clothing—and held it out to Rayne.

Rayne didn't move.

She stared at the woman. Then at the cloak. Then back at the woman. Her eyebrow lifted—slowly, deliberately, a question and a challenge all at once. A smirk tugged at the corner of her mouth, small and private and entirely her own.

Then she bowed.

It was a shallow thing, her back barely dipping, her head barely lowering. But her arms swept out to her sides, her palms open, her fingers spread—a gesture that was equal parts courtesy and mockery.

"Madam," she said.

Her voice was soft. Sweet. Dangerous.

The woman's expression flickered again—something cold, something sharp—but she didn't rise to it. She simply turned her back on Rayne, dismissing her entirely, and faced the windows once more.

Rayne straightened.

She didn't say anything. Didn't do anything. She simply backed away—slowly, deliberately, her eyes fixed on the woman's back, her smirk still in place. One step. Two. Three. Then she turned and walked back down the carpet runner, the cloak draped over her arm.

Her back was to Nikara now. Her green eyes found her friends—Garrick and Corin and Maren—and her smirk widened into something bright and sharp and entirely too pleased with itself. They were staring at her, their mouths hanging open, their faces frozen somewhere between disbelief and awe.

A door opened.

It was a side door, set into the wall near the dais—a small thing, barely noticeable, its wood dark and unadorned. It swung open with a soft click, and through it, into the throne room, stepped a man.

King Lucas was tall and broad-shouldered, his hair the same deep red as Rayne's, though it was streaked now with grey at the temples. His face was weathered and kind, his green eyes bright with intelligence, his beard trimmed short and neat. He wore a crown of gold and iron—simple, unadorned, the kind of crown a man wore when he had no need to prove his rank.

He stepped into the room, and his eyes swept across the crowd.

Then he saw the woman standing before the throne.

His face lit up.

"Nikara!" he called, his voice warm with genuine delight. He started toward her, his steps quick and eager. "I was hoping you'd arrive early. It's good to see you."

The woman—Nikara—turned from the windows, and her smile was a different thing entirely from the cold, sharp expression she'd worn moments before. This smile was warm. Real. It crinkled the corners of her blue eyes and softened the hard lines of her face.

"Lucas," she said, and her voice was warm too, rich with affection. "It's been too long."

"Far too long." He reached her and took her hands in his, squeezing them gently. "How is your mother? Still terrorizing the council back home?"

Nikara laughed—a real laugh, bright and clear. "She's well. And yes, she's still terrorizing them. I think she considers it her personal mission to make at least one councilor resign every year."

"Good for her. The world needs more women like your mother."

"I'll tell her you said so. She'll be insufferably pleased."

They talked on, their voices low and easy, the kind of conversation that came from years of friendship. Around them, the room continued as it had been—the laughter and the chatter and the clink of glasses, the servants moving among the guests, the nobles and merchants and soldiers going about their business. No one paid the king and his guest any particular mind. They were just another conversation in a room full of them.

No one noticed Rayne.

She had stopped halfway down the carpet runner, her back still to Nikara, her green eyes still fixed on her friends. Garrick was grinning now, his broad face lit with delight. Corin was shaking his head, but he was smiling too. Maren just stared, her expression caught somewhere between horror and admiration.

Rayne turned left.

She walked toward the fireplace—a massive thing, set into the wall on the left side of the room, its stone carved with dragons and serpents and stars. A fire burned within it, bright and hot, its flames dancing and crackling and throwing shadows across the floor.

And as she walked, she turned her head.

Her green eyes found Nikara's back. Fixed on it. Held it.

Her father noticed. King Lucas's eyes flicked to her, just for a moment, even as he kept talking to Nikara. His brow furrowed, just slightly. But he didn't stop her. Didn't call out. He just watched, his eyes tracking her as she walked across the room.

Rayne stopped before the fireplace.

She didn't look at the fire. She didn't look at her father. She didn't look at anyone.

She stared at Nikara.

And then she threw the cloak into the fire.

The flames caught it instantly. A great burst of fire exploded upward—a whoosh of noise and heat and light that made the whole room flinch. Sparks flew. Shadows danced. The fire roared, bright and hungry, and the cloak curled and blackened and burned.

Nikara started to turn.

But King Lucas caught her attention—a hand on her arm, a question in his voice, something that made her look back at him instead of at the fire. She didn't see it. Didn't see the cloak burning. Didn't see Rayne standing before the flames, her green eyes fixed on her, her smirk still in place.

The soldiers—Garrick and Corin and Maren—were staring. Their mouths hung open, their eyes wide. And then Garrick started to laugh—a low, delighted chuckle that shook his broad shoulders. Corin joined in, his smile breaking free. Maren shook her head, but she was laughing too, quiet and disbelieving, her eyes bright with something that might have been admiration, or might have been disbelief, or might have been both.

Rayne turned from the fire and walked back toward Nikara and her father.

King Lucas saw her coming. His eyes flicked to her, then to the fire, then back to her. Something crossed his face—a flicker of exasperation, maybe, or resignation, or the weary acceptance of a father who had long since stopped being surprised by his daughter's antics.

"—and this," he said, his voice carefully smooth, "is my daughter Rayne. Rayne, this is Nikara."

Nikara turned.

Her smile dropped from her face.

For a moment, she just stared at Rayne—her blue eyes wide, her lips parted, her expression frozen somewhere between shock and something that might have been fear. Then she recovered. Her smile returned—smaller now, tighter, edged with something that wasn't quite warmth.

"Oh," she said. "Rayne."

She held out her hand.

Rayne looked at it.

She looked at it the way she might look at a dead fish, or a stain on a rug, or something unpleasant she'd found on the bottom of her boot. Then she looked at Nikara's face. Then back at the hand.

She didn't take it.

"Uh—" Rayne said, her voice flat, her green eyes still fixed on Nikara's face. "Nice to meet you, Nikara."

She didn't wait for a reply.

She turned to her father. "Can you excuse me, Father? I want to go check on Neve."

King Lucas's eyes flicked between the two women—Rayne's flat, hard stare, Nikara's frozen smile—and something in his face shifted. Weariness, maybe. Or resignation. Or the quiet acknowledgment that his daughter had just declared war in the middle of his throne room.

"Of course, daughter," he said.

Rayne nodded once. Then she turned and walked away—past the dais, past the throne, past the stained glass windows. She walked toward the side door her father had come through, the small one set into the wall near the dais, its wood dark and unadorned.

She pushed it open and stepped through.

It closed behind her with a soft click.

And the room went on, as rooms do, as if nothing had happened at all.
