# Chapter One: The White Guardian

The forest glimmered beneath the morning sun. Golden light filtered through the canopy and dappled the moss and wildflowers, birds called overhead, and the air smelled of damp earth and something ancient.

Through that quiet beauty came the pounding of bare feet, small, dirty feet that had run so long they barely felt the stones and roots cutting into them.

The girl crashed through a curtain of hanging vines, her breath coming in ragged gasps. Her hair, once a neat braid, had come loose and wild, tangled with leaves and sweat. Her dress, a simple cotton thing that might have been yellow once, was torn at the hem and smeared with mud.

She didn’t look back. Looking back meant slowing down, and slowing down meant—

A howl split the air behind her.

It was not an ordinary wolf’s howl. Three voices were layered into one, a dissonant harmony that seemed to come from everywhere at once, and it crawled down her spine like ice water.

She ran faster. Oak and birch blurred past, then something with thorns that caught her sleeve and tore it, and she wrenched free without slowing and left a scrap of yellow fabric behind like a flag of surrender. Behind her something large moved through the forest, no longer bothering with stealth. It knew its prey was tiring. Branches cracked like gunshots as it shouldered through the undergrowth.

Her foot caught on a root and she stumbled, scraped her palm against a tree trunk, and kept going. Blood streaked her hand and she barely noticed. Fear drove her on long after her body begged her to stop. A growl rolled through the trees, low and long and hungry, and she veered left, ducked a branch, nearly collided with an enormous spiderweb, twisted away, lost her footing on the uneven ground and slid down a small slope on her back in a shower of dirt and leaves. At the bottom she scrambled up again.

Keep going. Keep going. Don’t stop. Don’t—

Another howl. Much closer.

She burst through a final wall of ferns and into a clearing.

It was a perfect circle of grass ringed with violet, gold and pale-pink wildflowers. Sunlight pooled on it like honey, and tiny blue butterflies drifted through the light, their wings flashing like fragments of stained glass. It should have felt peaceful. But there were no trees to climb and no rocks to duck behind, only open ground and open sky and the creeping realization that she had made a mistake.

She sprinted across it anyway. Her foot caught in a shallow hollow hidden in the grass and she went down hard, the air leaving her lungs, the world going white with pain. She rolled onto her back and tried to push herself up.

Her ankle screamed.

Her foot was wedged in the hollow, a burrow maybe, or just a gap in the earth, and when she pulled, pain shot up her leg like lightning. No, no, no. She yanked harder, twisting, fighting, her vision blurring, until she heard it: something entering the clearing.

She stopped moving. Slowly, trembling, she looked up.

Three creatures emerged from the treeline, hulking shapes that seemed to swallow the sunlight. Their grey-black fur hung in matted clumps, their eyes burned like coals, and their mouths were unnaturally wide, crowded with teeth. Each had three thick tails that twitched with a life of their own.

They stopped at the edge of the clearing. They didn’t charge. They already knew how this would end.

The girl’s breath came in sharp gasps. Tears cut tracks through the dirt on her cheeks. She pulled at her trapped foot again, but it wouldn’t budge.

The wolves, if they could be called that, began to circle. Their claws clicked against the grass and their growls rumbled like distant thunder. One licked its lips, showing teeth no natural creature should possess. They had been hungry for a long time, and at last they had found their prey.

A sob escaped her, small and broken. She pressed herself against the ground as if she could sink into the earth and disappear.

Then she raised her arm.

Her hand shook, a fine, uncontrollable tremor that ran from her fingertips to her elbow. She pointed her palm at the wolves, jaw clenched, brow furrowed with desperate concentration.

For a moment nothing happened. Then a flicker: a tiny burst of pale blue-white light crackled off her fingertips like sparks from a dying fire. It danced in the air for half a heartbeat, bright and fragile, and fizzled out.

The wolves didn’t even flinch. The lead wolf tilted its head, watching with something that looked almost like amusement, its tails swaying.

“Please,” she whispered.

She tried again. She squeezed her eyes shut and poured everything she had left into her palm, into her fingers, into the spark that refused to catch, and nothing came. Her arm dropped to her side, limp and useless.

The largest wolf stepped forward, fur the color of storm clouds, eyes like dying embers. Its mouth stretched into something that might have been a smile. The girl squeezed her eyes shut as it advanced, claws sinking into the earth, the other two fanning out behind it while their growls vibrated through the ground. She couldn’t move, couldn’t breathe, couldn’t even force a scream from her throat.

The leader’s shadow fell over her. She could smell it now, rot and decay and something metallic, like old blood. Its jaws parted, strings of saliva stretching between teeth far too long and far too sharp.

It lowered its head.

And then a sound came from the forest behind the trees, not from any of the three wolves. It was a growl, deeper than their snarls, a sound that settled into bone and rattled teeth. There was anger in it so immense that the wolves’ hunger seemed almost polite by comparison.

The lead wolf froze, ears flattening against its skull. The other two stopped circling, their growls dying in their throats as they turned toward the sound.

For a long moment nothing moved.

Then two full-grown oaks, their trunks as thick as a man’s body, flew sideways as if they weighed nothing and crashed down on either side of the clearing, roots tearing free, leaves showering down like green rain. Through the gap they left behind came something white.

It was like the grey wolves, and it wasn’t. Its fur was bright as fresh snow. It stood taller and broader than its rivals, muscle shifting beneath its coat like coiled steel, and four tails streamed behind it, wreathed in a shimmering mist that pulsed with every beat of its heart. It launched itself into the clearing and landed between the girl and the three wolves, and the ground shook.

The girl felt the terror leave her. Her shoulders lowered and her breathing steadied, and the tears on her cheeks caught the sunlight. She couldn’t have explained how she knew, but she was safe, and the certainty settled over her like a warm blanket.

All three wolves recoiled. Not much, just a step, but it was enough.

The lead wolf recovered first. It was scarred and battle-worn, one ear torn nearly in half, and it studied the white wolf with something beyond hunger in its eyes. Calculation. It took a step forward, then another, head lowered, a growl rumbling in its chest: not a threat this time but a question.

The white wolf didn’t growl back. It stood with its four tails raised, the mist crackling around its body like heat lightning, and its golden eyes, bright as twin suns, locked onto the lead wolf with an intensity that made the air feel heavier.

The lead wolf hesitated with one paw hovering above the ground. Then it lunged.

The fight was brief and brutal. The leader went for the white wolf’s shoulder, but the larger creature twisted with startling speed and caught it by the neck, not hard enough to kill but hard enough to hurt. The leader yelped. The other two charged in, snapping and snarling, one at the flank and one at the hind legs. The white wolf released the leader and spun, catching the second attacker mid-leap and closing its jaws around a foreleg, and one sharp shake sent it tumbling across the clearing. The third faltered, and the white wolf seized the opening, slamming into the lead wolf with the force of a landslide. The two of them crashed through a tree at the edge of the clearing, the trunk splintering around them, and when the dust cleared the leader lay pinned beneath the white wolf’s massive paws, wheezing.

The white wolf lowered its muzzle until it was inches from the leader’s torn ear. It made a sound softer than a growl, a low, rolling murmur that sounded almost like words: a warning, and a promise.

The lead wolf understood.

The white wolf stepped back. The leader scrambled up, stumbling, favoring its left side, and looked at the white wolf, really looked, and for the first time the hunger in its eyes was gone. Defeat had replaced it. It turned and fled, and the other two followed, limping and whimpering, vanishing into the trees without a backward glance. The sound of their retreat faded until there was nothing left but rustling leaves and the distant call of birds.

The white wolf watched them go, then turned back.

The girl was still on the ground, her foot still wedged in the hollow, but she was no longer trembling. She looked up at the white wolf with wide eyes and a small, shaky smile.

The wolf padded toward her, and as it moved the mist around it brightened and swirled until she had to squint.

The mist folded in on itself, and where the wolf had stood, a young woman knelt in the grass. Her hair fell past her shoulders, deep red as autumn leaves. The mist left behind the smell of wild strawberries, sweet and sun-warm, the kind that grew in hidden patches deep in the forest, and the gold drained from her eyes until they were simply green, warm as sunlight through leaves. She wore a tunic of soft grey, trousers, and boots made for long walks.

A familiar young woman.

“Rayne,” the girl breathed.

Rayne’s hands were already on her sister, checking Neve’s face, her arms, the awkward angle of her leg. Her expression held relief and worry and, underneath, a good deal of exasperation.

“Hold still,” she said, her voice low and firm. She gripped Neve’s calf with one hand and the top of her foot with the other and worked the foot free of the hollow, so carefully. Neve bit her lip to keep from crying out, but the pain was brief.

Rayne probed the ankle with gentle, practiced fingers. “Not broken. Sprained. You’ve got some cuts that need cleaning, but nothing serious.”

She sat back on her heels, and for a moment her hands trembled. Then she pressed them flat against her thighs.

“Are you okay?”

“I’m fine,” Neve said, a small smile breaking through the dirt and tears. “My ankle hurts, but I’m fine.”

Rayne exhaled, slowly and deliberately. “Good. Because if you ever do that again, I’m going to kill you myself.”

Neve’s smile wavered, caught between relief and guilt.

“We’ve been looking for you for hours,” Rayne said, and her expression softened, then hardened again, the way an older sibling’s does when they’ve been scared half to death and are trying very hard not to show it. “Father is furious. Or he will be, once he stops being terrified. At the moment I suspect he’s managing both.”

Neve winced. “I know. I just—“

“Today of all days. The envoys from the eastern court arrive at sundown.”

Neve picked at the torn hem of her dress. “The wildflowers by the falls. The guards said they were blooming, and I’ve never seen them, and I thought if I was quick I’d be back before anyone noticed.”

“And before you got there, the wolves started chasing you.”

Neve nodded.

Rayne brushed a strand of hair from her sister’s face and tucked it behind her ear. “All right. Let’s get you home before Father sends out a search party large enough to qualify as an invasion. I’ll talk to him. Try to smooth things over.”

Neve’s face lit up, and she threw her arms around Rayne’s neck. “Thank you. I love you.”

Rayne hesitated for a heartbeat. Then her arms came up and she held her sister close.

“I love you too,” she said softly, and she held on a moment longer than necessary, as if Neve might disappear if she let go.

She helped Neve to her feet. Neve wobbled, her ankle protesting, and Rayne tucked an arm around her shoulders and took most of her weight.

And over her sister’s head, the question she had been pushing aside finally caught up with her. The grey wolves, the three-tailed ones that hunted in packs and left nothing but bones, belonged to the far north, to the frozen reaches and the snow-choked valleys at the kingdom’s edge. In all the years Rayne had patrolled these lands, they had never once been seen this far south. They didn’t migrate. They didn’t wander. They stayed where the cold was and the prey was slow.

So why were they here, in the heart of the southern wood, chasing a child through a clearing that should have been safe?

Something had changed, or was changing, and she didn’t know what. She filed it away. Later. Right now her sister needed her.

Together, the two of them started walking, out of the clearing and through the trees, toward home. Behind them the forest settled back into its quiet rhythm, and the blue butterflies drifted on through the light.


# Chapter Two: A Flame Is Lit

Verdale had been awake since before dawn, and by late morning it had worked itself into a roar: hammers and haggling, cartwheels on cobblestones, gulls wheeling overhead and complaining about all of it. It was the largest town in the kingdom, and it wore the title easily. Its streets were wide enough for a dozen carts to pass abreast, and the houses along them rose three and four stories, their timber frames gone dark with age, their plaster walls freshly whitewashed so that the whole town seemed to glow in the sun.

The bakers had been at their ovens since the blue-dark hours, and the first loaves of the day were long sold and eaten, replaced by new ones that steamed on their shelves and sent the smell of warm bread drifting down every lane. Shutters stood open on every shop. Silk hung in ribbons from the merchants’ awnings in every color a dyer could name, and beneath them sat spices in little wooden bowls, sharp and strange, pepper and saffron and things the townsfolk could only guess at. A tanner had laid out belts and boots and saddlebags in neat rows. A potter’s glazes shone like wet river stones. Apples were stacked in perfect pyramids, their skins still beaded with dew, and beside them a fishwife’s cart glittered with silver scales, and beside that a flower stall overflowed with red and gold and violet, so bright it looked as though someone had spilled a paint box across the street.

And everywhere there were people. They moved in a great, churning river between the stalls, merchants and farmers, travelers and townsfolk, children darting between the grown-ups’ legs and elders picking their way along the edges. A blacksmith’s hammer rang out in steady rhythm. A seamstress sat in her upstairs window, her needle flashing as she worked a gown of deep blue velvet. A young juggler kept five painted balls turning in a lazy circle while a crowd tossed coins into the cap at his feet, and nearby a puppeteer sent a little wooden dragon swooping above the upturned faces of a dozen children, who gasped every time it dove. An old woman with a face like a wrinkled apple called out her prices in a voice worn smooth by decades of haggling, and nobody believed a word of them, and everybody paid.

It was the kind of town where nothing bad had ever happened, and nobody there doubted that nothing bad ever would. Nobody there had seen a three-tailed wolf.

The main road ran down through the middle of it all, past the forge and the seamstress’s window, past a group of wool merchants arguing good-naturedly over a price, past a young couple stealing a kiss in the shade of an awning, past a dog chasing a cat chasing a pigeon, and it kept on going until the buildings fell away and the whole sea opened out in front of it.

The port of Verdale curved along the coast like a smile, a great crescent of stone quays and wooden piers, and the harbor inside it was deep blue and restless, chop-tipped with white wherever the wind caught it. It was crowded. Merchant ships rode at the long quays with their sails furled and their decks heaped with crates and barrels and bolts of cloth. Private yachts lay between them, sleek and elegant, their hulls gleaming with varnish and brass. Fishing boats, sturdy and weathered, had spread their nets across the stones to dry, and the whole waterfront smelled of salt and tar and fish and rope.

The docks were a hive. Laborers hauled crates and rolled barrels and shouted to one another in a dozen dialects. Sailors sat cross-legged on coils of rope, mending nets and arguing about the weather. Merchants stood with ship captains, haggling over the price of cargo with the strained patience of people who had done it a thousand times. Oars dipped, sails snapped, hulls knocked gently against the piers as boats came in and went out, and from somewhere out on the water a horn called, long and low, to say that another ship was ready to leave. Under it all ran the steady pulse of the sea, waves slapping stone and ropes creaking in their rings.

Past the docks and the harbor, the land rose.

It was a broad green hill, rounded and gentle, thick with forest on its slopes and crowned with a bare outcropping of pale grey stone. It was not quite a mountain. Centuries of wind and rain and the slow patience of the sea had made it softer than that. On the crown of it sat the castle, its towers and walls and pennants catching the late morning light, its pale stone glowing against the blue sky, and the sea ran along two sides of it and broke white against the rocks far below.

The road wound up the hillside in long, gentle switchbacks, paved in smooth grey stone and edged with low walls of fitted rock. Oak and pine grew thick on either side, and among them a tree with silver leaves that rustled softly in the sea breeze. The air cooled as the road climbed. The gulls grew louder, and the salt smell stronger, and the roar of the town thinned behind until it was only a murmur at the foot of the hill.

At the top the road leveled out, and the castle’s front gate stood across a deep chasm that had been cut into the hill itself. The drawbridge that spanned it was enormous: wide enough for four or five wagons to cross side by side, long enough that eight could have lined up nose to tail with room to spare. Its planks were thick oak, banded with iron, and they rang softly under the hooves of the occasional rider. Chains, each link as thick as a man’s forearm, ran from the bridge up into the gatehouse, ready to haul it up at a moment’s notice.

No one was hauling it today. The bridge was down and the gates stood open, because the castle was expecting company.

Beyond the gatehouse, the courtyard opened out like a held breath let go. It was a wide expanse of swept stone, so broad that it seemed to swallow the light, and everything in it hummed with the orderly noise of a place that ran well. To the left stood the guard quarters, long low buildings of the same pale grey stone as the walls, their doors propped open to the morning air. Beyond them was a mess hall, its chimney already smoking, the smell of onions and roasting meat drifting out across the stones, and scattered among them were smaller buildings, offices and storerooms whose purposes were known only to those who worked within.

To the right ran the stables, a long row of stalls with their half-doors open and horses stamping and nickering in the shadows, and a stable boy was walking a grey mare in slow circles, murmuring to it. Next to the stables stood a forge where a hammer rang and sparks leaped from the anvil in bright arcs. Beyond it was the weapons repository, its doors guarded by two soldiers in crimson tabards, and past that lay the training rings, circles of packed earth ringed with low stone walls, where soldiers drilled and sparred and shouted in the morning sun, their wooden swords cracking together like branches in a storm.

At the seaward edge of the courtyard, the ground fell away to two private ports.

The first belonged to the military fleet. Its warships were long and dark and low in the water, their decks crowded with ballistae and catapults and rows of shielded soldiers, and they lay at anchor in neat, orderly lines with their banners snapping in the wind and their crews moving across the decks with the unhurried efficiency of long familiarity.

The second port lay further along, close beside the castle itself, and it was kept for royal guests and special arrivals. Its quay was swept clean. Its mooring posts were bound in brass. And in the finest berth of all sat a ship unlike any other in the harbor.

It was beautiful. The hull was lacquered white as seafoam and so freshly varnished that the water beneath it was mirrored in its sides, and a band of deep blue ran along the gunwale, trimmed with gilded scrollwork that curled like breaking waves. The decks were teak, scrubbed pale and polished until they glowed. The brightwork was brass, the rails were hand-rubbed, and the stern held a row of tall windows of leaded glass, behind which the soft shapes of lanterns and silk drapes could be glimpsed. A carved figurehead leaned out from the prow, a sea dragon with a long, finned neck and pearl-white scales, its mouth open in a silent, joyful roar and its eyes set with mother-of-pearl. Its sails were furled, but even furled they were magnificent, white and blue and woven so fine that they seemed to shimmer, and worked across the canvas in shades of blue that deepened and lightened with every fold was a second sea dragon, serpentine and finned, rising from a curl of waves with its jaws parted and its eyes bright and fierce and alive. It was a ship the size of a merchant vessel, and there was a grace in its lines, an elegant, unhurried power, that made it look as if it had been built for a king.

Which, of course, it had.

But small things were wrong, for anyone who looked long enough, and no one was meant to. The ship sat a hand’s breadth lower in the water than a yacht of that size should have. The crew on its decks were too many and stood too still, and their hands, resting on the rail, were scarred in ways that rope alone does not scar them. The carved panels along the bulwark, with their pretty scrolls of waves and shells, were hinged, and behind each one, folded flat and oiled, waited a ballista. Beneath the polished decking, the timbers had been reinforced with iron ribs, and under the waterline, hidden by the sea itself, the hull was sheathed in plates of dark iron bolted to the frame. Under the prow, where the sea dragon’s neck met the bow, a heavy copper-sheathed ram lay tucked beneath the waves like a secret. The ship was a warship dressed as a luxury yacht, and its beauty was a mask.

Out in the harbor, at a close but decent distance, other ships sat at anchor, their sails furled, their decks quiet. They looked like merchantmen. They were not. They watched the water the way a hawk watches a field, patient and still and entirely ready.

In the center of the courtyard, where the stones were laid in a wide circle, stood a fountain.

It was a dragon, though not a monster or a beast out of nightmare. This was something older and quieter, a dragon of the earth, carved from stone the color of moss and riverbed. Its body was low and powerful, its wings were folded against its back, and its tail curled around the base of the fountain like a cat’s. Its head was raised and its jaws were open, and from them poured a steady stream of water, clear and bright, scattering the sunlight into a thousand tiny rainbows before it fell into the wide basin below. Around the rim of the basin ran a line of letters, worn smooth by time, in a language no one remembered anymore. Pigeons bathed on the edge. Children from the guard families flicked water at one another when the sergeants weren’t looking.

Behind the fountain the ground rose, and broad, shallow stairs of pale stone swept up in a gentle curve toward the castle, flanked by low walls whose tops had been polished by centuries of passing hands. At the head of the stairs, set into the castle’s front wall, was the door.

It was taller than three men and twice as wide, made of wood so dark it looked like old honey, polished to a gleam that caught the light and held it. Wide iron bands crossed it, their edges worked with patterns too fine to see from a distance. Where the two halves met, a dragon’s head was carved from the same dark wood, its features sharp and fierce, its eyes closed, its jaws just parted. When the doors were shut it was a single creature keeping watch over the threshold. When they opened, the face split down the middle, two halves of the same dragon drawing apart to let the world inside.

The doors stood open now, and the castle took in the day’s visitors.

Inside, the air was cool and still, carrying the smell of stone and candlewax and something older, something that had been part of the walls so long it had seeped into the mortar. The entry hall was wide and high, its ceiling lost in shadow, its floor laid with pale tiles in an elegant interlocking pattern, worn soft at the center by generations of footsteps.

It was only a passage. Beyond it, through another set of doors thrown wide, their hinges singing softly, lay the throne room.

It had two levels. The lower was a floor of polished stone, so smooth it held the light like dark water. The upper was a marble-balustraded walkway that ringed the whole room, its arches opening onto shadowed corridors that ran deeper into the castle, and guards in crimson tabards stood along it at intervals with their spears upright and their eyes on the floor below. At the far end of the hall, behind the throne, the windows rose almost to the ceiling: stained glass in a thousand colors, so old that no one remembered who had made it or why. There was a dragon with its wings spread, a mountain with its peak crowned in fire, a river winding through a forest of silver trees, and above them a sky of stars, their light caught in glass and held there forever. The sun poured through in great shafts of gold and crimson and blue, and scattered across the floor in shifting jewel-colored light.

Before the windows, on a dais of pale stone, stood the throne. Its dark wood was banded with iron and its back rose high above the head of anyone who sat in it. The arms were carved as dragon claws, their talons curling around the ends of the rests, and the legs as scaled feet gripping the dais. Above it, worked into the wood, was a dragon’s head with its jaws open and its eyes bright with something that might have been gold. Two smaller chairs of crimson velvet and gilded frames stood on either side, a little lower, a little plainer. Not thrones, exactly. But close.

The room was full of people. They stood in groups across the polished floor like islands in a dark sea: nobles in silks and velvets with jewels catching the light, merchants in plainer cloth with calloused hands and sharp eyes, soldiers in crimson and gold, relaxed in their posture and never still in their eyes. Servants moved among them with trays of wine and small, delicate pastries, and the air was full of the sound of conversation, laughter, the clink of glasses, the murmur of voices rising and falling like the tide.

Near the doors at the back, four people stood together: three soldiers and Rayne.

Maren, the tallest, had cropped dark hair and a sharp, amused face. Garrick and Corin were brothers, with the same broad faces and the same easy way of standing, though Garrick was elder and louder and laughed first, and Corin said little and watched everything. Rayne stood among them with her red hair loose, barely into her thirties but carrying herself like someone older, and she was really laughing, the kind of laugh that crinkled her eyes.

“—and then he said, ‘That’s not a chicken, that’s my wife!’” Garrick finished.

Maren snorted. “You’ve told that story a hundred times. It wasn’t funny the first time.”

“It was hilarious the first time. You’ve no sense of humor.”

“I have plenty. I just don’t laugh at the same joke for a decade.”

“I thought it was funny,” Rayne said.

“Thank you, Rayne. At least someone appreciates good comedy.”

“Good comedy? That was a series of words arranged in the shape of a joke.”

“That’s philosophy, that is.”

“The chicken had better timing,” Corin said, and Garrick clutched his chest.

None of them noticed the doors at the back swing open, or the young woman who came through them.

She was barely into her twenties, with long blonde hair in loose waves and sun-warmed skin, as though she had spent her life under a brighter sky than this one. She wore midnight blue, cut to leave her shoulders and arms bare, with light armor stitched so neatly into it that it was hard to tell where cloth ended and plate began, the kind of protection worn by someone who intended to dance through a fight. At her wrist, half hidden by her sleeve, was a bracelet of small beads, wood or polished stone, their colors faded. It looked like something she had worn for a very long time. She wore no crown and no jewels, nothing that said who she was.

She walked straight down the carpet runner toward the throne without slowing, and Rayne, still laughing, was standing in the middle of it.

The woman’s shoulder struck hers hard enough to turn her half a step. She didn’t break stride or glance back, the way you might pass a coat stand.

Rayne turned, the laughter gone from her face.

“Rayne,” Corin said, already stepping forward, his hand on his sword. “Want me to remove her?”

Rayne raised one finger toward the ceiling. Corin stopped, jaw tight, and his hand fell to his side.

She followed the woman down the runner, unhurried, her back straight, past the nobles and servants and the guards on the walls, all the way to the dais. The woman stood with her back to the room, gazing up at the windows. Rayne stopped a pace behind her and cleared her throat.

The woman turned. “What?”

Rayne said nothing. She looked at the woman the way a hawk looks at a field mouse, expressionless, her arms loose at her sides.

The woman’s glare faltered, and then she gave a small, impatient sigh, as if the answer had occurred to her. She unfastened her cloak, a deep blue, the same as her clothes, and held it out.

Rayne looked at the woman, at the cloak, and at the woman again. One eyebrow rose, slowly. Then she bowed, a shallow thing, her back barely dipping, her arms sweeping out to her sides, palms open, in a gesture that was half courtesy and half mockery.

“Madam,” she said, soft and sweet and dangerous.

“Here,” the woman said, and threw the cloak over Rayne’s outstretched arms. Then she turned her back and went on gazing at the windows, as though Rayne had already left the room.

Rayne straightened, the cloak draped across her arms. She stood a moment, looking at the back of the woman’s head, her smirk small and private. Then she turned and walked back down the carpet runner, unhurried, one step and then another.

She stopped halfway down it. Her friends were watching her, and she looked at them, still smirking, the cloak hanging from her arms. Garrick frowned, puzzled. Maren tilted her head. Corin raised an eyebrow, a question in it, and none of them could tell what she meant to do.

A small side door beside the dais clicked open, and King Lucas stepped through. He was tall and broad-shouldered, his hair the same red as Rayne’s, streaked with grey at the temples, his beard short and neat, his green eyes quick. His crown of gold and iron was plain, the kind a man wears when he has nothing to prove. He swept the room with a glance, saw the woman before the throne, and his face lit up.

“Nikara! I was hoping you’d arrive early.” He crossed to her quickly and took her hands.

Her smile was a different thing from the one she had worn a moment before: warm and real, crinkling the corners of her eyes. “Lucas. You’ve gone grey.”

“You made me grey. Last time you arrived in a storm with a broken mast, and I told everyone it was a fine entrance.”

“It was a fine entrance.”

“It was a shipwreck.” He laughed. “How is your mother? Still driving the council to resign?”

“One a year, like clockwork. She says it keeps them honest.”

They talked on, low and easy, with the ease of old friends, and nobody paid them any mind.

Nobody noticed Rayne, either. She had turned left, away from her friends, and was crossing to the great fireplace in the wall, its stone carved with dragons and serpents and stars, a fire burning bright inside it. Her friends watched her go, their confusion deepening into curiosity, and Garrick took a half step after her before Maren caught his sleeve. As Rayne walked she kept her eyes on Nikara’s back, and her father, mid-sentence, saw it. He frowned, just slightly, but said nothing.

Rayne stopped at the hearth. She looked once at the fire and once at Nikara.

Then she threw the cloak into the fire.

The flames caught it at once. A great whoosh of sound and heat rolled out of the hearth as fire roared up the chimney in a bright, hungry column, sparks flying, shadows leaping across the floor, the cloak curling and blackening in the blaze.

Nobody noticed. The room was too full and too loud, and every guest was turned toward a friend or a glass or a story. Nikara began to turn, but Lucas touched her arm and asked her something about the voyage, and she looked back at him and missed all of it.

The only ones who saw were the three at the back of the room. They were staring, mouths open, eyes wide. Then Garrick finally understood, and he started to laugh, a low, helpless chuckle that shook his shoulders, and Corin joined him, and Maren shook her head and laughed too, quietly, like someone who had decided she would deny everything later.

Rayne walked back across the floor. Lucas saw her coming, looked at her, at the fire, and at her again, and something crossed his face, the weary acceptance of a father who had long since stopped being surprised.

“And this,” he said, smooth as glass, “is my daughter Rayne. Rayne, Nikara.”

Nikara turned. Her smile stayed up for a moment, and then it froze. She looked at Rayne, at the red hair and the green eyes, and at the king’s daughter standing in front of her. Her lips parted.

“Oh,” she said. “Rayne.” She held out her hand, her smile stiff and thin.

Rayne looked at the hand the way she might look at a dead fish. Then she looked at Nikara’s face, and back at the hand.

She didn’t take it.

“Nice to meet you, Nikara.” She turned to her father. “Will you excuse me, Father? I want to check on Neve.”

Lucas looked from his daughter’s flat stare to Nikara’s frozen smile, and some of the light went out of his face. “Of course,” he said.

Rayne nodded once and walked away past the dais and the throne and the great windows, toward the small door in the wall. She pushed it open and stepped through, then paused with one hand on the frame and turned her head, enough to look back over her shoulder.

“Oh, Nikara,” she said, light and sweet. “Do bundle up. Nights around here get chilly this time of year.”

The door closed behind her with a soft click, and the room went back to its wine and its murmuring, as rooms do.
